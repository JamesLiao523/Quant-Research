Portfolio construction research note

Nested Clustered Optimization

Grouping assets before optimizing removes most of the damage from noisy estimates when the truth is known. On ten years of real ETFs and stocks, the gain shrinks to a size luck alone could produce.

Summary

The study in brief

A portfolio optimizer needs the volatility of every asset and the correlation of every pair, and it treats these estimates as exact. Nested clustered optimization (NCO) groups assets that move together, optimizes inside each group, then optimizes across groups. The study first measures the gain in a simulated market where the true risks are known, then runs monthly long-only backtests on 22 ETFs and on 112 ETFs and stocks from 2017 to 2026.

In simulation, NCO cuts the excess risk of a minimum-variance portfolio from 22% to 7% and lifts a max-Sharpe portfolio from 43% to 67% of the best achievable. In real long-only portfolios the gain shrinks to 0.02–0.03 of Sharpe ratio, every bootstrap interval includes zero, and NCO trades up to 2.7 times as much as the plain optimizer. On the ETFs, a plain equal split (Sharpe 0.79) beats every optimizer.

Why it matters

  • A method's simulated gain is not a real-world gain. The same technique that removes two thirds of the excess risk in a clean test adds almost nothing once a no-short rule already limits extreme bets.
  • Stability on paper can turn into turnover in practice. With fixed groups NCO's weights move a third as much as the plain optimizer's; with groups re-estimated each month they move more.
  • Simple allocators are hard to beat. Equal weight, inverse variance and risk parity all beat every optimizer on the ETFs, with no estimates of returns at all.
  • It shows where NCO belongs. The large simulated gains come from unconstrained portfolios that use expected returns: long-short, leveraged and max-Sharpe books.

The problem · An optimizer believes every estimate

Picture a shopper who believes every price tag, including the misprinted ones. Whenever a tag looks too good, the shopper buys heavily. A portfolio optimizer behaves the same way. An asset whose risk looks low in the sample, or whose correlation with the rest looks low, gets a large weight, even when the low number is an accident of the sample.

The numbers involved are large. A portfolio of 40 assets needs 40 volatilities and 780 correlations. Ten years of monthly data give 120 observations of each asset, three per asset. In the simulated market used here, a true correlation of 0.5 is estimated anywhere between 0.28 and 0.67 from one such sample, and a true correlation of 0.1 anywhere between −0.16 and 0.34. The optimizer cannot tell which estimates are wrong, so it acts on all of them.

NCO limits the damage by splitting one large decision into several small ones. A company does the same with its budget: it gives each department a total and lets the department divide it. Each decision uses a few numbers that are well understood, and an error inside one department stays inside that department.

Research questions

  1. How much risk and return does estimation error cost, and how much does NCO recover, when the truth is known?
  2. Why does grouping help?
  3. Does the gain survive in real long-only portfolios, and is it larger than luck?
  4. What does NCO cost in trading and concentration?

The data · A simulated market, then real ETFs and stocks

Dataset 1: a simulated market with known risks

On real data the true risk of a portfolio is never known, so estimation error cannot be measured there. The simulated market fixes the truth:

  • 40 assets in 5 groups of 8. Correlation 0.5 inside a group and 0.1 across groups.
  • Monthly volatilities between 5% and 12%. Expected returns are 0.15 × volatility, so every asset has the same Sharpe ratio (return per unit of risk), about 0.5 a year.
  • 200 samples of 120 months each. From each sample the risks and returns are estimated, weights are built, and the weights are scored with the true risks and returns.

Dataset 2: real ETFs and stocks

Daily adjusted closes from Yahoo Finance, Jan 2015 to Oct 2026. Backtests start in Jan 2017. At each of 118 monthly rebalances, risks and returns are estimated from the previous two years (504 trading days), weights are set, and they are held for 21 trading days. First look · The data invent safe bets that do not exist

Before any optimizer is run, compare the true correlation matrix of the simulated market with one estimate from 120 months of data. The eigenvalues of a correlation matrix measure how much independent movement it contains; the smallest ones describe the combinations of assets that look calmest. An optimizer puts its weight exactly there.

The estimate looks reasonable square by square, yet it invents low-risk combinations that do not exist. An optimizer that minimises risk will find those combinations and load up on them. The findings below measure what that costs and how much grouping first recovers.

How it is measured

Six allocators, from no estimates at all to full optimization:

  • Equal weight. 1/N in each asset. Uses no estimates.
  • Inverse variance. More weight on calmer assets (weight proportional to 1/volatility²). Ignores correlations.
  • Hierarchical risk parity (HRP). Sorts assets into a tree by correlation and splits risk evenly between the two halves of each branch. Never inverts the covariance matrix.
  • Minimum variance. The mix with the lowest estimated risk. Uses every volatility and correlation, through the inverse of the covariance matrix.
  • Max-Sharpe. The mix with the highest estimated return per unit of risk. Uses expected returns as well, which are far harder to estimate than risks.
  • NCO. Groups assets by correlation, runs the chosen optimizer inside each group, treats each group as one combined asset, and runs the optimizer again across groups. The final weight is the weight inside the group times the weight of the group.

In model terms: minimum variance sets w ∝ Σ⁻¹1 and max-Sharpe w ∝ Σ⁻¹μ (long-only versions are solved numerically). NCO computes intra-group weights wk for each group k, forms the reduced covariance W′ΣW across groups, solves the same problem on it, and multiplies. Groups come from ONC (optimal number of clusters), which runs k-means on the distance √(½(1 − ρ)) for every possible number of groups and keeps the one where assets sit clearly closer to their own group. On real data the common market move is removed from the correlations first (detoning), so the groups reflect what assets share beyond the market.

Steps

  1. Check the building blocks. With three unrelated assets, inverse variance, HRP and minimum variance must give the same weights (0.59, 0.26, 0.15). They do.
  2. Simulate. 200 samples of 120 months from the known market; score each allocator's minimum-variance portfolio by its true volatility against the lowest possible, and each max-Sharpe portfolio by its true Sharpe ratio against the best possible. NCO runs with estimated groups and with the true groups; a cleaned (denoised) covariance is a second cure for comparison.
  3. Explain the gain. On one sample, compare how much each method's matrix amplifies errors (its condition number) and how far its weights move when one more month of data arrives.
  4. Backtest. 118 monthly rebalances from Jan 2017 on 22 ETFs and on 112 assets, long-only, two-year estimation window, 10 basis points of cost per unit traded. NCO is tested with ONC groups and with 8 fixed k-means groups.
  5. Test against luck. For each NCO portfolio and its plain counterpart, a moving-block bootstrap (21-day blocks, 2,000 resamples) gives a 90% interval for the difference in Sharpe ratio.
  6. Measure the cost. Turnover per rebalance (half the sum of weight changes), effective number of holdings (1/Σw², the number of equal positions the portfolio is equivalent to) and the largest weight.

Finding 1 · Grouping undoes most of the damage from noisy estimates

Each allocator is built 200 times from noisy samples and scored against the true market. A score of 1.00 means the allocator did as well as if the true risks had been known.

The grouping step itself is not the weak point: ONC finds 5.1 groups on average against a truth of 5, so NCO with estimated groups performs like NCO with the true ones. Max-Sharpe gains the most because expected returns are the noisiest input. In 0.5% of samples the plain max-Sharpe weights even flip sign, turning the best portfolio into its opposite; without those samples the plain average is still 43%, so NCO's gain does not come from avoiding that one failure.

Finding 2 · Smaller decisions leave less room for error

Minimum variance needs the inverse of the covariance matrix, and inverting a matrix magnifies the errors in it. The condition number measures by how much: 1 means no magnification, 132 means errors can grow up to 132-fold. NCO never inverts the full matrix, only one small matrix per group and one across groups.

Smaller, better-behaved problems mean that an error in one estimate stays inside its group instead of spreading into every weight. The stability measured here holds the groups fixed. Finding 4 shows what happens when they are re-estimated every month.

The simulated gain was large because the plain optimizer was free to take extreme long and short positions on noisy numbers. The no-short rule already prevents most of those bets, so there is little left for grouping to fix. A Sharpe gain of 0.02 over ten years cannot be told apart from luck.

Finding 4 · The stable method ends up trading more

Finding 2 showed NCO's weights moving a third as much with fixed groups. In the backtests the groups are re-estimated every month, and ONC's group count varies from 2 to 8 on the ETFs and from 2 to 11 on 112 assets.

The stability NCO shows with fixed groups does not survive monthly re-grouping: when an asset changes group, its weight is rebuilt from scratch. A long-only minimum-variance portfolio, plain or nested, ends up holding the equivalent of two assets, which is low risk on paper but little diversification.

How to use it

  1. Use NCO where estimation error is worst. Portfolios that use expected returns (max-Sharpe), long-short or leveraged books, and many assets with short histories. That is where the simulated gains were large (43% → 67% of the best Sharpe ratio).
  2. Test before adopting it for long-only portfolios. The no-short rule already does most of the protecting. Here NCO added 0.02 of Sharpe, within luck, while equal weight, inverse variance and HRP did better.
  3. Keep the groups stable. Refit them once a year, or only when they change clearly. Month-to-month label changes cost more trading than the gain is worth (0.133 vs 0.049 on 112 assets).
  4. Never run minimum variance on a universe with a near-cash asset. It will hold almost nothing else.

Denoising the covariance is an alternative cure for the same problem. In simulation it nearly matched NCO for minimum variance (8% excess risk against 7%) without any grouping step, and so without label-driven trading.

Use it when

  • The optimizer uses expected returns or can go short or leveraged.
  • There are many assets relative to the length of the history.
  • The assets fall into clear, stable groups (asset classes, sectors).

Skip it when

  • The portfolio is long-only minimum variance; test inverse variance and HRP first.
  • Groups would change often and trading costs matter.
  • A simple allocator already meets the risk target.

Conclusion

NCO does what it claims where the problem is sharpest. When an optimizer acts freely on noisy estimates, splitting the decision into groups keeps each error inside its group and recovers most of the lost performance. Real long-only portfolios are a milder problem: the no-short rule has already removed the extreme bets, and what remains for NCO to fix is smaller than the noise in ten years of returns and smaller than the extra trading its changing groups create. The method belongs in unconstrained, return-driven portfolios; for a long-only allocation, the simple rules are the benchmark to beat.

Limitations and next steps

  • The simulated market has equal, well-separated groups, the easy case for grouping. Uneven or overlapping groups would give NCO less to work with.
  • The real tests are long-only. The case where NCO should help most, long-short and leveraged portfolios, is untested on real data.
  • The stock list holds today's large caps, so stock-heavy returns carry survivorship bias; only comparisons between allocators are meaningful.
  • Next: long-short and leveraged real tests; groups refit once a year; a denoised covariance inside NCO.
