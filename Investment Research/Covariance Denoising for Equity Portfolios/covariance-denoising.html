Portfolio research note

Covariance Denoising for Equity Portfolios

Removing random noise from a stock correlation matrix makes minimum-variance portfolios much safer when data are scarce, and riskier when they are not.

Executive summary

The study in brief

A portfolio optimiser inverts the covariance matrix, so every estimation error in it is amplified into the weights. Random matrix theory gives the range of eigenvalues that pure noise would produce for a given amount of data; denoising keeps the eigenvalues above that range and flattens the rest. The study checks the theory on simulated data, applies it to 90 US large-cap stocks, and measures the realised volatility of minimum-variance portfolios built on the cleaned matrix against the raw sample matrix and Ledoit-Wolf shrinkage, from Jan 2017 to Oct 2026.

The answer depends on how much data the estimate has. With 6 months of returns (1.4 days per stock), denoising cuts realised volatility from 21.0% to 13.4% and turnover from 3.5 to 0.7 per rebalance. With 2 years, it raises volatility from 14.1% to 16.4%, because the noise cut-off keeps only 7 factors and throws away sector structure that 20–30 factors would preserve. Ledoit-Wolf shrinkage gives the lowest volatility at every window length.

All volatility figures are annualised and refer to an unconstrained minimum-variance portfolio rebalanced every 21 trading days.

Why it matters

  • Window length decides the estimator. Below about 3 days of data per asset, raw sample covariance is unusable for optimisation (21.0% volatility, weights turning over 3.5 times per month); a cleaning step is mandatory, not optional.
  • It prevents a common mistake. Applying the noise cut-off by default to a long equity window removes real sector information and adds 2.3 points of volatility. The cut-off is a lower bound on the number of factors, not the answer.
  • Cheaper to run. Denoised weights have the lowest turnover at every window length, a saving that compounds once trading costs are charged.
  • A safe default exists. Ledoit-Wolf shrinkage gives the lowest volatility at every window (13.3–13.7%), which makes it the right choice when the window length is not fixed in advance.

The problem · Optimisers amplify estimation noise

A minimum-variance portfolio puts weight on assets that diversify each other, and finding those weights requires inverting the covariance matrix. For 90 stocks that matrix has 4,005 distinct correlations. With 6 months of daily data each one rests on 126 observations, so many of them are mostly noise. Inverting a noisy matrix turns small errors into large, unstable bets: the optimiser loads up on pairs that only look like hedges by chance.

The amount of noise depends on the ratio of observations to assets, q = T/N. Random matrix theory says exactly how the eigenvalues of a correlation matrix built from pure noise spread out for a given q. Anything above that range is structure; anything inside it can be noise. Denoising uses this boundary:

  • Eigenvalues: split the correlation matrix into independent directions of co-movement, each with a size (its share of total variance).
  • Noise band: fit the range pure noise would produce (the Marcenko-Pastur distribution) to the observed eigenvalues.
  • Keep the signal: eigenvalues above the band stay as they are.
  • Flatten the noise: eigenvalues inside the band are replaced by their average, and the matrix is rebuilt.

In model terms: for N assets and T observations of pure noise, correlation eigenvalues lie in λ± = σ²(1 ± √(N/T))². σ² is fitted to the observed spectrum; the eigenvalues above λ+ are kept, the rest set to their mean (trace preserved), and the result rescaled to unit diagonal. Detoning additionally removes the first eigenvector, the market factor.

Research questions

  1. What does denoising do to a real equity correlation matrix?
  2. Does it give lower-risk portfolios when the true covariance is known?
  3. Does it give lower-risk portfolios on real stocks with a 2-year window?
  4. Does the answer change when the estimation window is shorter?

Each question is answered by one finding below.

Data and first look · How much of the matrix stands above noise

The data are daily adjusted closes for 90 US large-cap stocks in 10 sectors from Jan 2015 to Oct 2026 (2,973 daily returns), converted to simple returns. Sector labels follow a GICS-like mapping.

Before any cleaning, two pictures frame the problem. The first shows how wide the eigenvalue range of pure noise is for different amounts of data: the less data, the more "structure" noise alone creates. The second shows the ten largest eigenvalues of the real 90-stock matrix over the last three years (q = 8.4) against the upper edge of the noise range.

The noise edge is not sharply identified on equities. The fitted noise variance runs into its upper bound (σ² = 1), and a second fit that preserves the trace of the bulk admits 35 signal eigenvalues instead of 7. Real returns have fat tails and clustered volatility, which the pure-noise model does not describe. Whether eigenvalues 8 to 35 are noise or real sector structure is what decides the out-of-sample results below.

Methodology

The study runs in seven steps, from raw prices to the choice of estimator.

  1. Data. Daily adjusted closes for 90 US large-cap stocks, Jan 2015 – Oct 2026, converted to simple daily returns; stocks with gaps above 2% of days are dropped.
  2. Noise benchmark. The Marcenko-Pastur range is checked against 20 simulations of pure-noise matrices (100 assets) at q = 2, 5, 10 and 20.
  3. Spectrum. Eigenvalues of the last 756 days are fitted two ways: by matching a kernel density to the noise distribution, and by a trace-preserving fixed point. The leading eigenvectors are read by sector.
  4. Cleaning. Constant-residual denoising (noise eigenvalues set to their mean), targeted shrinkage (noise off-diagonals removed) and detoning (market eigenvector removed) are compared by condition number and by correlation within and across sectors.
  5. Known-truth test. 200 samples of 200 days from a known covariance (100 assets, 10 blocks, within-block correlation 0.5, q = 2). Minimum-variance weights from each estimator are scored by their true risk relative to the best possible portfolio.
  6. Real-data backtest. Every 21 trading days, the covariance is estimated on the previous 504 days and minimum-variance weights are held for the next 21 days, unconstrained and long-only, Jan 2017 – Oct 2026 (118 rebalances), against equal weight. The number of kept factors is then swept from 1 to 45.
  7. Window length. The same backtest is repeated with 126-, 252- and 504-day windows on one common out-of-sample period, recording realised volatility and turnover.

Finding 1 · Denoising calms the matrix; detoning exposes the sectors

The first test is what cleaning does to the real 90-stock matrix. Stocks are ordered by sector, so sector blocks appear along the diagonal. A well-behaved matrix for optimisation has a low condition number, the ratio of its largest to smallest eigenvalue, which measures how much the inverse amplifies errors.

Constant-residual denoising makes the matrix far easier to invert, at a price: the gap between within-sector and across-sector correlation shrinks from 0.233 to 0.186, so some real sector structure is lost. Targeted shrinkage keeps more of it (0.224) with a smaller gain in conditioning (109). Detoning widens the gap to 0.276, which suits grouping stocks, but market risk is real risk and stays in any matrix used to size positions.

Finding 2 · With a known truth, denoising beats shrinkage

A cleaner matrix only matters if it leads to better portfolios, and on real data the true covariance is never known. A simulated market with a known covariance settles it: 100 assets in 10 sector blocks, 200 days of data per sample (q = 2), 200 samples. Each estimator's minimum-variance portfolio is scored by its true risk divided by the risk of the best possible portfolio.

When returns really are a few clean factors plus Gaussian noise, the theory delivers: denoising removes most of the estimation damage and beats the standard shrinkage benchmark by a wide margin. Real equity returns are not that clean, which is what the next finding tests.

Finding 3 · With a two-year window, denoising backfires

The simulation favours denoising; real stocks may not. With a 2-year estimation window (q = 5.6), the denoised minimum-variance portfolio is the riskiest of the three estimators. The factor sweep shows why: the result depends on how many eigenvalues are kept, and the noise cut-off keeps too few.

Eigenvalues 8 to 30 sit inside or near the fitted noise band, yet they carry sector information stable enough to lower risk out of sample. The noise model treats them as noise because equity returns are fatter-tailed than the model assumes. Ledoit-Wolf, which shrinks every eigenvalue smoothly instead of cutting at a threshold, keeps that information and delivers the lowest volatility (13.7%).

Finding 4 · With short windows, denoising pays

Finding 3 used a long window, where the sample matrix is already fairly reliable. Shorter windows react faster to changing markets but leave fewer days per stock, which is where noise removal should matter most. The same backtest is repeated with 6-month, 1-year and 2-year windows over one common period.

With 1.4 days per stock the sample matrix is dominated by noise, and the noise cut-off removes exactly what hurts. As the window lengthens the sample matrix becomes reliable and the structure the cut-off deletes starts to matter more than the noise it removes. The crossover sits between 1 and 2 years for this universe. Ledoit-Wolf is the lowest at every window (13.3%, 13.7%, 13.7%), so it is the estimator to use when the window length is not fixed in advance.

Implications for practice

The choice of covariance estimator follows from how much data each estimate rests on.

  1. Count days per asset. Compute q = T/N for the estimation window before choosing an estimator.
  2. Below q ≈ 3, clean the matrix. Denoising or Ledoit-Wolf; the raw sample matrix is unusable for optimisation.
  3. Above q ≈ 3, prefer Ledoit-Wolf. If denoising is used, tune the number of kept factors out of sample instead of trusting the noise cut-off.
  4. Use detoning for structure, never for risk. It reveals sector groups for clustering; position sizing needs the market factor.

Use it when

  • The estimation window is short relative to the number of assets.
  • Turnover is costly and calmer weights are worth a little risk.
  • A few clean factors drive the universe, as in many multi-asset portfolios.
  • Preparing a correlation matrix for clustering (with detoning).

Skip it when

  • The window holds several years of data per asset.
  • The universe is single-country equities with many mid-sized sector factors.
  • The cut-off would be applied without checking the factor count out of sample.

Conclusion

Denoising is a sound idea applied with the wrong default. Its noise boundary is correct for Gaussian data and it does exactly what it promises when data are scarce: a 6-month minimum-variance portfolio goes from 21.0% to 13.4% volatility with a fifth of the trading. On equities with longer windows its fixed cut-off is too aggressive and deletes sector structure that lowers risk. Smooth shrinkage avoids that failure without giving up the short-window gains, which makes it the better default, with denoising reserved for short windows and turnover-sensitive mandates.

Limitations and next steps

  • One universe of 90 of today's large caps, so returns carry survivorship bias; estimators are compared on the same data.
  • No trading costs are charged; the turnover advantage is real but unpriced.
  • The known-truth test uses one block design with Gaussian returns.
  • Next: denoise and then shrink the noise block with a Ledoit-Wolf weight; test a multi-asset universe; price turnover with realistic costs.
