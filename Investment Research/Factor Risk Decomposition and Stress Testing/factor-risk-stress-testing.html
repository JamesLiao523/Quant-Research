Portfolio risk research note

Factor Risk Decomposition and Stress Testing

Hidden factor bets are a primary risk in long/short portfolios. Factor risk decomposition finds them and shows which ones pay. In this book, an unplanned sector bet was the largest factor risk.

Summary

The study in brief

A long/short book buys some stocks and sells others short, so market moves largely cancel. What remains is mostly factor risk, the exposure to shared forces such as interest rates, sectors or last year's winners. The study builds such a book from 90 US large caps, rebalances it monthly from 2010 to 2026, and examines it in two stages. Factor risk decomposition splits the book's risk into nine factors plus stock-specific moves, then checks which sources earned money. Stress testing applies past crises, hypothetical shocks, simulated bad months and a reverse stress test, then compares five ways to change the book.

In the test book described below, the decomposition shows that factors drove 70% of the risk. The largest, a tilt toward value stocks, came from the ranking score and was never chosen. The two intended bets earned almost nothing, and the book lost 2.5% a year. In the stress tests, the worst days come from long bank positions, and a 10% monthly loss needs only an ordinary reversal in recent winners. Ranking stocks within each sector cut the worst fall from −46% to −19%, though no improvement is large enough to rule out luck.

The test book

Every number on this page describes one test book, so the figures change from book to book while the method stays the same. Each month, 90 US large caps are scored half on 12-month momentum (recent winners score high) and half on stability (calm stocks score high). The book buys the 20 highest scores and shorts the 20 lowest, sizes each position by the inverse of its volatility, and holds +100% of capital long and −100% short. It is rebalanced monthly from Jan 2010 to Nov 2026, with 10 bp of cost per unit traded. The score is a simple, untuned rule, and the book's own return is not the subject. The study asks what the risk analysis reveals about the book and how much each risk-management choice changes its outcome.

Returns and losses are shares of the book's capital: −10% on a $100 million book is $10 million. A standard deviation (sd) is a typical-size move; a 2.2 sd month is unusual but not rare.

Why it matters

  • The decomposition exposes bets hidden in a balanced book. This book held zero net capital in the market, yet gained 0.56% for every 1% that value stocks beat growth stocks.
  • It shows which risk to keep, hedge or fix. A VaR cut removes the most volatile names whether or not they pay. Here the riskiest fifth of stocks lost money and the safest fifth made it.
  • The stress tests find the positions behind the worst days. Everyday risk came from growth-stock shorts such as IBM. The worst days came from five long bank positions.
  • It carries over to any hedged portfolio. The same two stages work for a market-neutral fund, a hedged sector book or a multi-asset portfolio.

The problem · Risk limits cut the wrong stocks

A long/short book holds two sides. It buys stocks it expects to rise (longs) and borrows and sells stocks it expects to fall (shorts). A simple example: buy $1 million of JPMorgan and short $1 million of IBM. If the whole market falls 10% and both stocks fall with it, the loss on JPMorgan is paid for by the gain on IBM. The book makes or loses money only on how the two stocks differ.

Risk teams usually summarise such a book with one number, its value at risk (VaR): the loss that is exceeded on only 1 day in 100. When that number is too high, the quick fix is to cut the positions that add most to it. Those are the most volatile stocks, and they are often exactly the positions the manager is paid to hold.

This book shows the trap. Its largest single source of risk is a short position in IBM: 6.4% of capital, 8% of the book's risk. A cut driven by VaR would start there. Yet the book's worst simulated days come from five long bank positions (Citigroup, MetLife, US Bancorp, JPMorgan, Morgan Stanley), and none of them is in the top eight. Cutting IBM would lower the risk number and leave the real danger in place.

The terms used on this page

Return and volatilityReturn is the gain in a year. Volatility measures how much returns swing; 16% a year means a typical year lands within about ±16% of its average.
Sharpe ratioReturn divided by volatility: how much is earned per unit of risk. The S&P 500 scored 0.71 over 2010–2026.
DrawdownThe fall from a previous high to a later low. A −46% worst drawdown means the book once lost nearly half of its peak value.
FactorA broad force that moves many stocks at once: the market, interest rates, oil, or a style such as "recent winners".
ExposureHow much the book gains when a factor rises 1%. An exposure of 0.56 to value means +0.56% when value stocks beat growth stocks by 1%.
Share of riskEach source's part of the book's total variance (volatility squared). The parts add up to 100%.
VaR and ESVaR 99% is the loss beaten only 1 time in 100. Expected shortfall (ES) is the average loss in those worst 1 in 100 cases.
Stress daysThe 10% of trading days with the most turbulent markets, measured by the S&P 500's volatility over the previous month.
Basis point (bp)One hundredth of a percent. 10 bp of trading cost is $1,000 on every $1 million traded.

The moving parts

  • Risk model: splits the book's risk into nine factors plus stock-specific moves.
  • Attribution: splits 16 years of returns the same way, to see which risks earned money.
  • Replays and scenarios: past crises and hypothetical shocks applied to today's positions.
  • Simulation: thousands of possible next months, drawn from history, to size a bad month.
  • Reverse stress test: the most likely way to lose a chosen amount.
  • Re-allocation: five changed versions of the book, compared over the same years.

In model terms. Each stock's daily return is ri = αi + Σk βik fk + εi: a sum of factor returns fk, each times the stock's sensitivity βik, plus a company-specific part εi. With weights w, the book's exposures are b = Bᵀw and its variance is bᵀΣb + Σi wi² di, where Σ is the factor covariance and di the specific variance of stock i. Each term of that sum is one source's share of risk.

First look · A neutral book with a hidden tilt, in a market that changes in crises

Two facts are visible before any risk model is run. The first is in the positions themselves. The book holds +100% of capital in longs and −100% in shorts, so it has no net money in the market. Grouping the same positions by sector tells a different story.

The second is in the market data. Volatility on stress days is compared with volatility on calm days for each of the nine factors used later. If crises only made markets a little jumpier, a risk model fitted on normal days would be good enough.

A book that is neutral in dollars can still be a large bet on one part of the market against another. And because crises make every factor jumpier and more connected, risk estimated on ordinary days will understate crisis losses. The rest of the study measures both effects and asks what to do about them.

Method · Eight steps from prices to a risk report

The study runs in eight steps, from raw prices to a one-page risk report. Each step names where to check it in the notebook (section number) and in the helper module risk_tools.py (function name).

  1. Data. Daily adjusted closing prices from Yahoo Finance, 3 Jan 2007 to 6 Nov 2026, for 90 US large-cap stocks in 10 sectors and 24 ETFs. Daily returns are percentage price changes. A stock enters once it has a year of history. Notebook setup cell · data_utils.load_prices
  2. Factors. Nine factor return series, eight built from ETF prices and momentum built from the stocks: every 21 trading days, the equal-weighted top third minus bottom third of the 90 stocks by 12-month return, skipping the latest month. Stress days are those whose trailing 21-day S&P 500 volatility is in the top 10% of 2008–2026. Section 1 · build_factor_returns, stress_days
  3. The book. Each month every stock gets a score: half its 12-month momentum, half its stability (low volatility over 126 days once the market's effect is removed), each standardised across stocks. Long the top 20, short the bottom 20; within each side, weights are proportional to 1 ÷ volatility over the past 63 days, scaled to +100% and −100%. Held 21 trading days, 10 bp cost per unit traded, from Jan 2010: 202 rebalances. Section 2 · book_scores, build_book, run_backtest
  4. Risk model. Each stock's daily returns over the past 252 days are regressed on the nine factors. The fitted sensitivities, the factor covariance and the leftover (specific) variance give the book's predicted volatility, its exposure to each factor, and each factor's and each position's share of risk. Section 3 · risk_model, risk_decomposition
  5. Paid or unpaid. Over 2010–2026 each day's book return is split into exposure × factor return for each factor plus a stock-specific remainder. Each part's average return ÷ its volatility shows whether that risk earned money. Stocks are also sorted into fifths by their share of risk while held. Section 4 · factor_attribution
  6. Crises and shocks. Today's positions are run through 13 past episodes and split into factor and specific losses; the book actually held at the time is shown too. Nine hypothetical shocks fix one or two factors and fill in the others with their average move given the shock, using correlations from all days and from stress days. Sections 5–6 · holdings_history, conditional_scenario
  7. Bad months and the 10% loss. Today's book is applied to daily returns since 2008, and 20,000 one-month paths are simulated four ways (bell curve, 5-day block bootstrap, filtered historical, filtered historical from stress days only) to measure the 99% VaR and ES. A reverse stress test finds the most likely factor move that loses 10% in a month. Sections 7–8 · bootstrap_paths, fhs_paths, component_es, reverse_stress
  8. Re-allocation and luck test. Five changed versions of the book are rebuilt monthly over the same period and compared on return, risk, worst fall and crisis losses. Each one's Sharpe gain over the base book is resampled 2,000 times in 21-day blocks to give a 90% interval. Section 9 · min_vol_weights, hedge_overlay, risk_budget_weights, sector_neutral_book

Checks that the numbers add up. The factor and specific parts of the 2010–2026 return add to −1.29% a year, the book's arithmetic return to the same digit. Shares of risk add to 100%, and so do the ES contributions of the positions. On one-day losses the bell-curve VaR (2.31%) and the historical VaR (2.26%) agree, which confirms the model's volatility is right in normal times.

Finding 1 · Factors carry 70% of the risk, led by a bet nobody chose

The risk model splits today's predicted volatility of 15.7% a year into one part per factor plus the stock-specific part. Each source is marked as intended (momentum and stock picks, the two bets the score was designed to take) or accidental (everything else). If the book were working as designed, the intended bets would dominate.

Why value? The stability half of the score favours calm stocks and penalises jumpy ones. Calm stocks cluster in banks, energy and consumer staples; jumpy ones in technology, media and retail. So a score with no sector in it builds a sector bet. Its exposure of 0.56 to value means the book gains about 0.56% each time value stocks beat growth stocks by 1%, and loses the same when they lag.

Finding 2 · The bets the book was built on did not pay

A large risk is fine if it earns money. Over 202 monthly rebalances from 2010 to 2026 the book lost 2.5% a year at 15.8% volatility (Sharpe ratio −0.16). To find which risks caused the loss, each day's return is split by source as in method step 5. A source earning above about 0.3 per unit of its own volatility is clearly paying its way; one near zero is risk without reward.

In this book the riskiest stocks were not the source of return. That changes the question a risk manager should ask. Cutting risk would cost nothing here, because no risk was paid; the useful question is which part of the book loses money. Finding 6 answers it.

Finding 3 · The book fears momentum reversals more than market crashes

A historical replay applies the daily returns of a past crisis to today's positions: what would 2008 do to the book held now? The loss splits into a factor part and a stock-specific part. A second column uses the book actually held at the time, which is what the strategy really lived through.

History offers only the crises that happened. A hypothetical scenario sets one or two factors to a chosen move, for example the market down 20%, and fills in the other factors with their average move on such a day, worked out from their correlations. Each scenario is run with correlations from all days and from stress days.

The book is built to win in the scenarios a market-risk report usually tests and to lose in the one it rarely tests: a sudden reversal in which last year's losers beat last year's winners. A replay library for long/short books needs momentum crashes, short squeezes and bank failures alongside market crashes.

Finding 4 · A bad month is deeper than the bell curve says

The bell curve (normal distribution) assumes large losses are very rare. Real markets have fatter tails: big moves come more often and in clusters. Four ways to estimate next month's P&L for today's book, from 2008–2026 history:

  • Bell curve: today's predicted volatility, normal distribution.
  • Block bootstrap: real past days drawn in runs of 5, so fat tails and short losing streaks stay in.
  • Filtered historical simulation: each past day is divided by the volatility of its own time, then multiplied by today's volatility. A calm 2017 day and a wild 2020 day both become a day at today's level, with their own shape.
  • Filtered, stress days only: the same, drawing only the shocks of stress days. These are the days when volatility jumped faster than its recent average, so their rescaled shocks are larger.

The stocks that decide the worst days are not the ones a volatility ranking flags. A limit on tail risk should be set on each position's ES contribution; a cut driven by volatility would remove the wrong stocks.

Finding 5 · The most likely 10% loss is a momentum reversal

A reverse stress test starts from the loss instead of the scenario. It asks: of all the combinations of factor moves that would cost the book 10% in a month, which is the most likely? For a book whose P&L is its exposures times the factor moves, the answer has a closed form, and its size in standard deviations says how unusual that month would be.

In model terms. With exposures b, monthly factor covariance Σ and loss L, the most likely move is f* = −L·Σb ÷ (bᵀΣb). It needs L ÷ √(bᵀΣb) standard deviations, and its monthly probability is the normal tail beyond that.

Finding 6 · Removing the accidental bet does the most good

Five ways to change the book, each rebuilt monthly from 2010 and compared with the original:

  • Minimum volatility: the same 40 stocks, re-weighted to the lowest predicted volatility, no position above 15%. This is what a pure risk-cutting rule does.
  • Hedged: the original book plus a basket of factor ETFs that cancels every factor exposure except momentum, the intended factor bet.
  • Conviction-sized: the same stocks, weighted so each position's share of risk matches the strength of its score.
  • Sector-neutral rebuild: the same score, ranked within each sector: long the 2 best and short the 2 worst stocks in every sector, so sector bets cancel by construction.
  • Sector-neutral and hedged: the sector-neutral book plus the same ETF hedge.

Implications for practice

  1. Split the risk before acting on a VaR number. Break the book's risk into factors and stock-specific moves, and mark each source as chosen or accidental.
  2. Judge each source by what it earned per unit of risk. Keep and size the chosen bets that pay. A large risk that pays is the source of return; a large risk that does not is a cost.
  3. Remove accidental bets where they are created. Here ranking stocks within sectors removed more risk than an ETF hedge on top, at the same turnover. Hedge with ETFs only what cannot be fixed at the source.
  4. Set tail limits on ES contributions. The positions that drive the worst days can be completely different from those that drive everyday swings.
  5. Test with crisis correlations and long/short crises. Replay momentum crashes, short squeezes and bank failures, run scenarios with stress-day correlations, and use a reverse stress test to name the scenario behind an unacceptable loss.

Use it when

  • Writing the monthly risk review of a long/short book.
  • Deciding what to hedge before an event.
  • A risk limit is breached and the question is which positions to cut.
  • Explaining why a risky position should stay, or why a calm one should go.

Skip it when

  • The book holds options or other positions whose P&L is not proportional to the market move.
  • Positions are hard to sell quickly; the cost of exiting in a crisis is not modelled.
  • Most holdings have less than a year of daily prices, so exposures cannot be estimated.

Conclusion

The toolkit turns one risk number into decisions. For this book, the decision it points to is structural. The riskiest stocks lost money, so cutting risk would have helped, and the idea that risky positions are the return source did not hold. More important, the risk that hurt was created by the ranking itself: a score built from calm stocks and recent winners quietly bet on banks and energy against technology. Fixing the ranking cut the worst fall by more than half; hedging the result with ETFs did little. Risk management can remove risk that is not paid. It cannot create a return the signal does not have, and the best version of this book still earned only about 1% a year.

Limitations and next steps

  • The stock list holds today's large caps, so returns carry survivorship bias; comparisons between methods are what matter.
  • The book has no positive edge, so it cannot show the case where risky positions earn their risk. A book with a working signal and sector limits inside the optimiser is the next test.
  • Factor sensitivities come from one year of daily data, and the model has no sector factors; the value factor partly stands in for them.
  • Scenarios assume P&L proportional to factor moves, with no cost of exiting. Days needed to sell each position, from trading volume, would add that cost.
