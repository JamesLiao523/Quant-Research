Options research note

Pricing the WTI Smile

A simple curve with three numbers, refitted each morning, prices every WTI option strike to within about a cent of the market in normal conditions.

What problem this solves

An option model needs one key input: how much the price is expected to move, called volatility. The textbook models (Black and Bachelier) use a single volatility for every strike. The market doesn't. On an average day in this sample, a far out-of-the-money WTI put was priced as if oil were 38% volatile, a far call at 33%, and the at-the-money option at 31%. Plotted against strike, those numbers form a curve traders call the smile.

The usual workaround is to plug a different volatility into the model for each strike. That gets the quotes right, but the book is then priced under dozens of models that contradict each other. That causes trouble when pricing anything that spans several strikes, such as spreads and digitals, and when deciding how much to hedge.

The quadratic normal model (QNM) solves this with one model for all strikes. It lets volatility change with the oil price along a parabola, a U-shaped curve described by three numbers:

  • Level: how high volatility sits overall.
  • Slope: whether volatility rises more when oil goes up or when it goes down.
  • Curvature: how sharply volatility rises for big moves in either direction, which is what makes far-out options expensive.

The model has a pricing formula that is quick to compute, so the three numbers can be refitted to the market every day with a simple regression.

In model terms: futures follow dF = σ(F) dz with local volatility σ(F) = σA + a + bF + cF², where σA is the at-the-money normal vol in $/bbl. The price is the Bachelier price plus one correction term, and it is linear in (a, b, c), so each daily fit is ordinary least squares.

Goal

Answer four questions on three years of WTI options. Does one daily fit price every strike? How many days does a fit stay accurate? Do the three numbers mean anything a trader can use? Does the model price digitals better or hedge better than the textbook models?

Use it when

  • Pricing a book of options across many strikes and you want one consistent model behind all of them.
  • Pricing trades that depend on several strikes at once, such as spreads and digitals.
  • Looking for mispriced strikes. A quote that sits off the fitted curve while its neighbours sit on it stands out.
  • The asset moves in dollars rather than in percent, as oil and interest rates tend to.

Skip it when

  • You can't refit it every day. A fit goes stale within a few days.
  • The trade lives in the far tails (beyond the 10-delta options). The formula under-prices those.
  • You want to predict where oil is going, or to hedge better in calm markets. It does neither.

Results summary

All numbers are for the third-listed contract, two to three months from expiry. "Pricing error" is the typical gap between the model price and the market price per option, in cents per barrel. One cent per barrel is $10 on a standard 1,000-barrel contract.

1.3¢ typical miss, 2023–25

In normal markets the model prices options within about a cent. A single Black volatility misses by 18–24¢ and a single Bachelier volatility by 16–17¢.

8.7¢ typical miss, 2026

In the 2026 rally, when call prices jumped, the fit got worse but stayed far ahead of Black (59¢) and Bachelier (121¢).

46¢ if not refitted for 5 days

The fit goes stale fast. After a week without refitting it misses by 46¢ (Black 70¢, Bachelier 95¢). Refit every day or two.

No gain in hedging, 2023–25

Hedging with the model's deltas left about the same risk as hedging with Black deltas: a ratio of 0.98, with a 90% range of 0.94–1.04.

The main takeaway is the pricing. The model is a reliable way to put one consistent price on every strike, as long as it is refitted daily. It does not forecast: its probability that oil ends higher was no more accurate than a coin flip. The hedging advantage over the full sample comes from five 2026 expiries, where hedging more aggressively in both tails paid off during a strong trend. Five expiries is too few to treat that as reliable.

Over the full sample a single Bachelier vol looks worse than a single Black vol (58.5¢ vs 33.1¢), but that comes from 2026 alone. In ordinary markets normal vol is the better base, and the QNM cuts the remaining error by a factor of 12 to 16. The errors also stop depending on strike. A single vol of either kind leaves a clear pattern across strikes; the QNM leaves almost none.

What the parameters say

The parabola can be written by its vertex (a variance shift A and a centre F0) or by its level, slope and curvature at the current futures price. The vertex form is unstable: by definition A = ℓ − c(F − F0)², and whenever curvature is small the centre F0 = F − s/2c runs off towards infinity. Across the sample, A correlates 0.998 with −c(F − F0)², driven by those extreme days. Level, slope and curvature stay finite and map onto the straddle, risk reversal and strangle.

Curvature falls with maturity (median 0.072 for m2, 0.046 for m3, 0.033 for m4): longer horizons carry thinner tails. The low-convexity floor (c ≥ 0.001) never binds, because prices stayed above $55; a linear smile is more likely in low-price markets. For monitoring a book, the slope is the parameter to watch. It identified the 2026 regime change weeks before the curvature fell.

Where the formula is accurate

The closed form is an approximation, so it is checked against a full numerical solution of the same pricing equation (implicit finite differences on a grid that extends below zero, since a normal-type process can go negative). The test case is a symmetric parabola centred on a futures price of 80, with σA = 30 $/bbl.

Between strikes 60 and 100 the three-month closed form is within 1.8¢, and the one-year version is within 3.5¢ at every strike. A single Bachelier vol misses by 20–90¢ over the same range. The weak spot is the far three-month wing: at strikes 40 and 120 the closed form prices the OTM option at 14¢ against a true 37¢. The daily calibration stops at the 10-delta options (about ±19 dollars at three months), inside the accurate zone. Deep OTM digitals and tail options would inherit the wing error.

Digital options

A digital pays $1 if the futures price finishes above the strike. Its value is the market's risk-neutral probability, the negative strike derivative of the call price, and the QNM gives it in closed form. At the money it simplifies to 0.5 − √(τ/2π)·s/2: the slope alone moves it away from 0.50. Bachelier always prices it at 0.50, and Black always below.

The QNM digital is a consistent price that respects the smile, which matters when quoting digitals against vanilla hedges. It is not a forecast.

Delta hedging

The QNM delta adds hedge ratio in both tails relative to Bachelier, because it assigns more probability to large moves. The test sells a 25-delta put, a 25-delta call or an ATM straddle about 60, 53, 46 and 39 days before each expiry and hedges daily with futures until expiry: 420 positions over 35 expiries. Black and Bachelier deltas use each strike's own implied vol, re-read daily; QNM deltas use the day's single fit.

In 2023–25 (30 expiries) the three rules are indistinguishable: QNM-to-Black 0.98 (0.94–1.04) and QNM-to-Bachelier 1.01 (0.97–1.06). In the five 2026 expiries QNM error is 13–25% lower, because hedging both tails harder pays during a strong trend. Those intervals still include 1, except for the 25-delta put against Bachelier. Over the full sample there is no statistically reliable hedging advantage.

Conclusion

As a pricing model the QNM does what a desk needs. One fit per day and expiry reprices the liquid strikes to about a cent in ordinary markets and stays an order of magnitude better than a single Black or Bachelier vol in a stressed one. Its parameters, read as level, slope and curvature at the money, are the straddle, risk reversal and strangle in model form, and the slope flags a regime change clearly.

Its value is consistency, not prediction. It doesn't forecast direction, and it doesn't hedge better in ordinary markets; its tail-heavy delta behaves like a momentum overlay that pays only in trends. The practical uses are a single, coherent price for every strike and payoff in a book, and a clean residual for relative value.

Limitations and next steps

  • Three years and four expiries per day. The longest contract is about four months, so the maturity findings stop there, and the 2026 hedging result rests on five expiries.
  • The closed form under-prices the far wings by up to 60% at three months. Tail-sensitive payoffs need a numerical solution or a wider calibration window.
  • Settlement prices, not tradable bid-ask quotes, and a flat 5% rate.
  • Next: trade the residuals (z-score each strike's fit error and test mean reversion), fit level, slope and curvature as smooth functions of maturity, and run the same pipeline on heating oil, gasoline, natural gas and Treasury options.
