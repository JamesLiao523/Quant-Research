Volatility research note

Implied Vol vs. Models Through an Oil Shock

In the 2026 oil shock, the option market read the danger before any model did, then kept pricing it long after the peak had passed. That second habit is where the models win, and where selling volatility paid about twice the usual premium. A desk that tries to switch between the two in real time gets fooled by false alarms, which is why simply trusting option prices still beats every blend.

Summary

The study in brief

Volatility forecasts sit behind almost every decision a commodity risk desk makes: how much to hedge, where to set limits, and whether options are cheap or expensive. Two sources compete. Implied volatility is read from option prices and carries the market's view of what is coming. Statistical models such as GARCH-X learn from moves that have already happened. This study follows WTI crude from June 2025 to May 2026, through the 2026 oil shock, to see which source is right in each phase of a shock and whether a desk could switch between them in time.

The option market had both the best read and the worst habit. While a shock was building, its one-week forecast error was two to nine times smaller than any model's. After the peak it stayed nervous, and GARCH-X became the better forecaster. That overstay is also an opportunity: selling volatility in the fading phase earned +6.3 vol points a week, against +3.0 at other times.

Knowing the phase in advance would cut one-week forecast error by about 12%, but no real-time rule got any of it. Four switching blends all lost to plain implied vol, and the reason is clear in the signals. They do flag shocks early, yet they also light up on a quarter to more than half of ordinary days, so every switch pays for its false alarms. Over a month there is nothing to switch for, since implied vol wins in every phase. The main limit is the sample: almost every shock date comes from one episode.

Why it matters

  • It tells a desk when to doubt the option market. Option prices are the best guide as a shock builds, but they stay too high after the peak. That is the moment to lean on a model for limits, and to look at selling volatility.
  • It puts a price on regime switching. Perfect timing is worth about 12% of one-week forecast error and nothing over a month, which caps what any switching model can deliver.
  • It prevents a common mistake. A blend that beats a weak model can still lose to implied vol. Here every blend beat plain HAR and none beat the option market.
  • The method carries over. The hindsight benchmark and the signal scorecard work for any set of forecasts and any asset with listed options.

The problem · The best forecast seems to change with the market

Volatility is how much a price swings, quoted as a yearly percentage. Oil at 30% volatility moves about 1.9% on a typical day; at 90% it moves about 5.7%. Anyone who sells options, sets a risk limit or sizes a hedge needs a forecast of that number for the days ahead.

Two kinds of forecast compete. Implied volatility is read from option prices, so it reflects what traders expect, including news that has not reached prices yet. Statistical models such as GARCH and HAR learn from past price moves, so they react to what has already happened. Earlier work on the same data found that implied vol is the better forecast on average. Averages can hide a pattern, though. Option traders may react fastest when a shock begins, and then keep prices high for too long after it peaks. If so, a forecaster that knew which phase the market was in could switch to whichever source wins in that phase.

The difficulty is that the phase is only obvious afterwards. A switch has to decide in real time, using signals that may be late or noisy. This study measures how much switching could gain, then tests whether real-time blends gain any of it.

The terms used on this page

Realized volatilityHow much oil futures actually moved over the next 5 or 20 trading days, stated as a yearly percentage. This is what every forecast tries to predict.
Implied volatilityThe volatility priced into options today. It is the option market's forecast, and it usually carries a small premium paid to option sellers.
GARCH-X and HARModels that forecast from past price moves. GARCH-X also takes implied vol as an input; HAR uses past daily, weekly and monthly volatility.
Market phaseOnset: a calm stretch just before a spike. Fading: volatility is high but about to fall. Calm: everything else.
QLIKEThe standard loss for volatility forecasts. Zero means perfect; it punishes under-forecasting more than over-forecasting.
BlendA forecast that mixes several others with weights that change with market signals, learned only from the past.

The moving parts

  • Six forecasts: implied vol, a rescaled implied vol, EWMA, HAR, HAR with implied vol, and GARCH-X.
  • Four blends: a fixed mix, a hard switch, a smooth logistic weight and a regression with interactions.
  • Phase labels built from the future, used only to grade forecasts afterwards.
  • A hindsight benchmark that always knows the phase, to measure the size of the prize.

In model terms. The target is realized variance over the next h days, RV²t,h = (252/h) Σi=1..h r²t+i, with r the daily log return of the same futures contract. A forecast F is scored with QLIKE = RV²/F − log(RV²/F) − 1. A blend forecasts wt·FA + (1 − wt)·FB, where the weight wt depends only on signals known at time t.

The questions

Does each phase have its own best forecaster? Can a real-time blend beat implied vol? How much would perfect timing be worth? Do the signals see the phases coming? Does the answer change over a month? And can an option seller use the fading phase?

First look · Option prices and realized moves trade places through a shock

Before any model is fitted, the raw data already show the pattern this study tests. The chart puts the 20-day implied vol on each date next to the volatility that actually followed over the next 20 trading days.

In calm years the two lines run close together, and implied vol is the better guess. Around the 2026 shock they trade places twice. First the option market is too low as the move begins, then it stays too high once the move is fading. That suggests a forecaster who knew the phase could switch sources and do better. Whether anyone can know the phase in time is the real question.

Methodology

The study runs in seven steps, from raw prices to a measured verdict on regime switching.

  1. Data. CME WTI front-month settlements and option summaries, 1,299 trading days from March 2021 to May 2026. Returns compare the same contract on both days, so a roll never looks like a price jump. Days without a settlement are left out, never filled. The test runs from 2025-06-26 to 2026-05-22, 229 forecast dates.
  2. Target. Realized variance of daily returns over the next 5 and 20 trading days, stated per year. Every forecast is made with data up to the day before the window starts.
  3. Forecasts. Implied vol matched to each horizon, a rescaled implied vol fitted on past windows, EWMA, HAR, HAR with implied vol, and GARCH-X. New models are refitted every 21 trading days. For one-week forecasts the nearest option expiry is usually further out, so 76% of one-week implied vols are an approximation.
  4. Phases. Each date gets a label from what happened next: onset when a high-volatility stretch followed a calm one, fading when volatility was high but about to fall, calm otherwise. The labels grade forecasts and never enter one.
  5. Blends. A fixed mix with one weight set before the test (0.84 for a week, 0.69 for a month), a hard switch that waits a few days before switching back, a smooth logistic weight, and a regression with interactions. Each learns only from forecasts made in the past.
  6. Scoring. QLIKE and squared error on the dates where every forecast exists: 193 for one week and 180 for one month. Differences are tested with Diebold-Mariano tests and with 2,000 block-bootstrap resamples. Forecasts also set one-day risk limits, which are checked against real returns.
  7. Timing analysis. A hindsight switch that always picks the phase's best forecast measures the size of the prize. A scorecard grades each real-time signal on how well it spots onsets and how often it fires on ordinary days. A simple rule for the fading phase, set in advance, and an option-selling test complete the study.

Finding 1 · Each phase of a shock has its own best forecaster

The chart follows the main forecasts through the 2026 oil shock, with the onset and fading phases shaded. It shows the pattern from the first look on the forecasts themselves.

The reason is intuitive. Option traders price news as it arrives, so implied vol jumps the moment a shock starts, while models built on past moves must wait for the moves to happen. After the peak the roles reverse. Traders keep paying up for protection, and implied vol stays high even as daily moves calm down, so a model that tracks the actual moves does better. Over the whole period these effects net out, and implied vol still has the lowest loss.

Finding 2 · No real-time blend beats the option market

If each phase has a winner, a blend that leans on the right forecast at the right time should beat any single forecast. Four blends try, each learning only from the past. They are scored on the same 193 one-week dates as the single forecasts.

The blends also gave no early warning. Each forecast was turned into a one-day risk limit. In the 2026 shock the first limit rose 15 trading days after the shock began, and it came from implied vol itself. GARCH-X followed after 17 days and the other forecasts after 19 to 20. The hard switch and the logistic blend never reacted at all. A switching model built to catch shocks did worse at catching this one than the plain option price.

Finding 3 · Perfect timing is worth 12%, and the blends get none of it

To size the prize, the study builds a hindsight switch. On each date it uses whichever forecast did best in that date's phase. No real forecaster can do this, so its loss is the most that any switching rule could gain. Each real forecast is then scored by the share of that gain it captures.

The prize is about 12% of one-week forecast loss, from 0.259 down to 0.227. Every real-time forecast misses it, because whatever each one gains in the fading or calm phases it gives back at onsets, where implied vol is two to four times better.

A simple rule set in advance shows why switching is hard. It leans fully on GARCH-X when past-month volatility is high and implied vol is at least 10% below its recent peak. It helped while the first shock faded, but it stayed on through the April 2026 re-spike and swapped implied vol for GARCH-X just as volatility jumped again. A switch has to know when to switch back, and that is as hard as knowing when to switch in.

Finding 4 · The signals see the shock, but they cry wolf

If the blends fail, either the signals cannot see the phases or the blends do not use them well. Each real-time signal is graded on two things: how well it ranks onset dates above calm dates, and how often it is on during ordinary days. A useful alarm scores high on the first and low on the second.

So the information is there. Option-market signals do rank shock onsets well, and over a month they moved weeks before the 2026 shock. The trouble is that they are on for a third to more than half of ordinary days, which makes them a stress gauge rather than an alarm. The blends then waste what they have: the logistic weight scores 0.89 as a signal, yet it stays between 0.8 and 1.0 almost all the time and barely moves into the shock.

Finding 5 · Over a month there is nothing to time

The same tests at the 20-day horizon give a cleaner answer. Here implied vol is the best forecast at onset, the best in calm markets, and ties GARCH-X while a shock fades. The hindsight switch scores 0.1475 and implied vol 0.1476, so perfect timing would gain nothing.

The longer horizon removes the advantage models had while shocks faded. A month is long enough for the option market's view of the coming weeks to matter more than the moves of the last few days. For one-month risk numbers, implied vol is the forecast to use in every phase.

Finding 6 · The fading phase may pay an option seller

If implied vol stays too high while a shock fades, selling volatility in that phase should pay better than selling at random. The profit of a short variance position over the next week is approximated as (IV² − RV²) ÷ (2 × IV), in vol points.

Selling in the fading phase earned about twice the usual profit. It is the most promising lead in the study, and also the weakest evidence: 12 independent weeks, all from one episode, with a 65-point loss inside the same state. It is an idea to test on new data with real option prices, not a strategy.

Implications for practice

  1. Use implied vol as the core forecast. It had the lowest loss over both horizons and reacted first when the 2026 shock began.
  2. Lean toward a model only after a spike, and only for short horizons. GARCH-X beat implied vol while a shock faded over one week, and tied it over one month.
  3. Build the exit before the entry. Hand back to implied vol as soon as implied vol starts rising again; the simple fading rule lost because it stayed on through a second spike.
  4. Test a blend against implied vol and a fixed mix. Beating a weak model such as plain HAR says nothing; every blend here did that and none beat the option market.

Use it when

  • Choosing a volatility input for a one-week hedge or risk limit.
  • Deciding whether a regime-switching model is worth building.
  • Judging whether option prices look rich after a spike.

Skip it when

  • The horizon is a month or longer; implied vol is enough.
  • The market has no liquid options to give an implied vol.
  • A decision rests on catching a shock in advance; no signal here does that reliably.

Conclusion

Volatility forecasts do fit some market phases better than others, and the pattern has a clear cause: option traders react first and let go last. But the prize for timing it is small and short-lived, about 12% of one-week forecast error and nothing over a month. The signals that could guide a switch see shocks well but fire far too often, and the blends barely act on them. For a desk, the practical answer is to trust the option market by default and to treat the period after a spike as the one place where a model, or an option sale, may deserve a closer look.

Limitations and next steps

  • One stress episode supplies almost every onset and fading date, and the test period had been studied in earlier work. A locked holdout after May 2026 is the next test.
  • The one-week implied vol is an approximation on three dates in four. Weekly options would give a true one-week benchmark.
  • The option-selling profit is a variance-swap approximation without costs, margin or real option prices.
  • WTI parameters were also applied unchanged to natural gas; that transfer and option-market onset signals with fewer false alarms are the natural follow-ups.
