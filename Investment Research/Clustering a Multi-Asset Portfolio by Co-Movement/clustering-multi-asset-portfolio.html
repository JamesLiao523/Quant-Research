Portfolio research note

Clustering a Multi-Asset Portfolio by Co-Movement

Grouping assets by how they actually move works well once the market factor is removed, but the automatic choice of how many groups to form is too fragile to trust on its own.

Executive summary

The study in brief

The question was whether returns alone can sort a portfolio into meaningful groups without anyone deciding how many groups there should be. Correlations between 23 ETFs and 90 stocks were turned into distances, and a clustering method that picks the number of groups by itself (optimal number of clusters, ONC) grouped them. Its choices were checked three ways: against economic sense, against simulated portfolios where the right answer is known, and against itself over rolling 2-year windows.

The grouping works and the counting does not. With the market factor removed, the ETFs fall into 8 groups that read like a portfolio manager's own map: US growth, US value, developed, emerging, safe bonds, credit with real estate, metals and energy. The number of groups is the weak link. ONC under-splits whenever some groups are stronger than others, finding 2 groups among 90 stocks, and its count jumps between 2 and 7 from one window to the next. With the count fixed at 5, consecutive windows agree at an ARI of 0.84, against 0.45 when ONC picks the count.

Agreement between two groupings is measured by the adjusted Rand index (ARI): 1 means identical groups, 0 means no better than random assignment.

Why it matters

  • Hidden concentration shows up. SPY, QQQ and VUG look like three holdings and trade as one. HYG and EMB carry bond labels but sit with equities on raw correlation. Risk measured by label understates both.
  • Risk budgets can follow behaviour. Limits set per behavioural group ("at most 30% in US growth") track what actually moves together, and the groups update as relationships change.
  • It prevents a common mistake. Feeding an automatic cluster count into an allocator or risk system would rebuild the groups, and the portfolio, every year. The study shows where that breaks and gives a fix: detone, propose k with ONC, cross-check, then hold k fixed.
  • The recipe carries over. The same steps (distance, detoning, a stability check) apply to grouping features, signals or strategies before they are scored or combined.

The problem · Labels hide how a portfolio really moves

A portfolio of 20 ETFs or 90 stocks is easier to manage as a handful of groups: risk budgets per group, diversification checks across groups, or an optimiser that works group by group. Labels such as "US equity" or "bonds" are the usual way to form the groups, but labels do not always match behaviour. High-yield bonds (HYG) and emerging-market debt (EMB) carry a bond label, yet over this sample they move with equities.

Clustering forms groups from the returns themselves: assets whose returns move together land in the same group. The most common algorithm, k-means, needs the number of groups k in advance, and picking k by hand brings back the judgement clustering was meant to remove. The optimal number of clusters (ONC) method chooses k from the data:

  • Distance: turn each correlation into a distance, so assets that move together are close.
  • Silhouette: score how well each asset fits its own group compared with the nearest other group.
  • Choice of k: try every k and keep the partition where assets fit best and most evenly.
  • Repair: re-cluster any group that fits worse than average, and keep the change only if fit improves.

In model terms: distance d = √(½(1 − ρ)); silhouette S = (b − a) / max(a, b), with a the mean distance to the asset's own cluster and b to the nearest other cluster; partition quality = mean(S) / std(S), maximised over k and over random k-means starts; below-average clusters are re-clustered recursively.

Research questions

  1. Does ONC find groups that make economic sense on real ETFs?
  2. Does it recover groups that are known in advance?
  3. Where does it break, and does cleaning the correlation matrix help?
  4. Do the groups last from one period to the next?

Each question is answered by one finding below.

Data and first look · What the correlations show before any clustering

The data are daily adjusted closes from Jan 2015 to Oct 2026 (2,970 trading days) for two universes: 23 multi-asset ETFs covering US and international equity, government and credit bonds, metals, energy and real estate, and 90 US large-cap stocks across 10 sectors. Returns are simple daily returns.

Before any method is applied, the raw data already frame the problem. 23 ETFs give 253 pairwise correlations: far too many numbers to read as a picture of a portfolio, and the reason a grouping is needed at all. The first question is whether correlation captures how these assets depend on each other, or misses links that a broader measure would see.

Correlation measures straight-line co-movement only. Mutual information measures any dependence, curved or not: how much knowing one return tells about the other. Comparing the two on known relationships shows what correlation can miss; comparing them on the real ETF pairs shows whether that matters here.

On daily liquid assets, correlation carries nearly all the dependence information, so it is a sound basis for grouping; non-linear measures earn their cost on option-like payoffs. Correlation is converted to the distance √(½(1 − ρ)), which passes the triangle inequality on all 1,771 ETF triples (1 − ρ fails 151). What remains is the real problem: compressing 253 numbers into a handful of groups without choosing in advance how many there are.

Methodology

The study runs in seven steps, from raw prices to a judgement on whether the groups can be trusted.

  1. Data. Daily adjusted closes for 23 ETFs and 90 US large-cap stocks, Jan 2015 – Oct 2026 (2,970 days), converted to simple daily returns. Assets with gaps above 2% of days are dropped.
  2. Distance. Each pair's return correlation ρ becomes a distance d = √(½(1 − ρ)). The triangle inequality is checked on every ETF triple, and mutual information is compared with correlation to test whether non-linear links would change the picture.
  3. Cleaning (optional). Detoning removes the first principal component of the correlation matrix, the market factor. Denoising keeps only eigenvalues above the Marcenko-Pastur noise band and flattens the rest. Four inputs are compared: raw, detoned, denoised, both.
  4. Clustering with ONC. Each asset is described by its row of distances to all others. k-means runs for every k from 2 to a maximum with several random starts; the partition with the highest silhouette t-statistic, mean(S)/std(S), is kept. Clusters below average quality are re-clustered recursively, and the change is kept only if average quality rises.
  5. Validation on known groups. Simulated returns with six planted groups, equal and unequal in size and strength, at 100 to 1,000 days, 20 draws each. ONC and its variants are scored against k-means and Ward linkage with k chosen by the best mean silhouette.
  6. Comparison on real data. The groups found on ETFs (full history) and stocks (last 3 years) are compared with asset-class and sector labels by the adjusted Rand index, and the clustered correlation matrices are inspected for economic sense.
  7. Stability. ONC is re-run on 2-year windows stepped by one year. Each partition is compared with the previous one and with a k-means partition at a fixed k = 5; shuffled labels give the zero line.

Finding 1 · Removing the market reveals eight behavioural groups

With correlation settled as the input, the first test is whether ONC finds groups that make economic sense. On raw correlation ONC picks 4 groups (silhouette quality peaks at k = 4, about 4.2 against 2.7 at k = 2 and below 1.9 for k ≥ 5): one risk-on block, Treasuries, metals and energy. The market factor lifts every correlation together and hides the finer structure. With it removed (detoning), ONC picks 8.

#Group and members
 The 8 detoned ETF clusters. Agreement with asset-class labels rises from 0.30 (raw, 4 clusters) to 0.63.

The low agreement on raw correlation (0.30) is mostly a label problem. The labels split equities by region and file credit under bonds, while the returns say all equities and credit move together. Detoning removes that common move, and what remains are region, style and asset-class differences.

Finding 2 · The automatic count fails when groups differ in strength

The ETF groups look right, but on real data the true answer is unknown, so looking sensible is not proof. Simulated portfolios with planted groups provide a test where the answer is known. Each asset loads on its block factor with within-block correlation ρ. With six equal blocks (ρ = 0.3) every method is perfect from T = 100 days. The hard case has six blocks of sizes 4 to 20 and ρ from 0.08 to 0.45: weak, small groups next to strong, large ones.

The failure is in the stopping rule, not in the data. The base step puts the strong blocks into one tight group and the weak blocks into one loose group. Only that one group scores below average, and the plain rule re-clusters only when two or more are weak, so it stops at 2. Allowing a single weak group to be re-clustered, recursively, fixes most of it. On this design, scoring partitions by mean(S)/std(S) adds nothing over the plain mean silhouette.

Finding 3 · Detoning helps ETFs; stocks stay at two groups

The simulation predicts trouble whenever groups differ in strength. Sectors are exactly that case, so the 90 stocks are the real-data test of that prediction. Two cleaning steps are also tried on both universes: removing the market factor (detoning) and removing estimation noise (denoising).

On ETFs, detoning is what matters (0.30 → 0.63). Denoising and detoning together collapse to 2 groups: denoising keeps only a few eigenvectors, and once the first is removed the next factor, bonds against everything else, dominates. On stocks, ONC sees 2 groups on every input (roughly cyclical against defensive, ARI 0.12–0.13). k-means told the true k = 10 reaches 0.43, so sector structure is in the data; ONC under-splits it, as in the unequal-block simulation.

Finding 4 · The number of groups is not stable over time

A grouping is only useful if it lasts: one that changes every year cannot anchor a risk budget. ONC is re-run on 2-year windows stepped by one year (detoned ETF correlation), and each partition is compared with the previous one. k-means with k fixed at 5 is the reference, and shuffled labels give the zero line.

Most of the instability comes from the choice of k, not from the correlations: with k fixed, the same assets keep landing together. The shuffled null scores 0.00, so even the unstable ONC partitions carry real information.

Implications for practice

The findings turn into a simple working rule: let the data decide which assets belong together, and let judgement, checked across time, decide how many groups there are.

  1. Detone first. Remove the market factor so style, region and asset-class structure becomes visible.
  2. Propose k with ONC. Use its count and members as the starting point.
  3. Cross-check the count. Compare with k-means or Ward at the best mean silhouette; if they disagree, the groups differ in strength and ONC is likely under-splitting.
  4. Fix k once it is sensible and stable. Hold it across rolling windows and let the data reassign members as relationships change.

Use it when

  • Building risk-budget or diversification groups for a multi-asset portfolio.
  • Checking whether asset-class labels match how assets actually trade.
  • Grouping correlated features or signals before scoring them.
  • The market factor has been removed first (detoning).

Skip it when

  • Groups differ a lot in strength, as sectors do. ONC then merges the weak ones into one block.
  • The number of groups has to stay fixed through time. The automatic k moves between 2 and 7.
  • The links are non-linear and correlation is the only input. Use an information-based distance instead.

Conclusion

Clustering on detoned correlation is a reliable way to see how a multi-asset portfolio is really grouped. It separates style, region, credit and commodity exposures that labels blur, and it does so with a distance that is mathematically sound. The weak link is the automatic number of groups. ONC's criterion favours coarse splits whenever group strengths differ, which is the normal case for sectors and stocks, and its count jumps between windows.

Used as a proposal for which assets belong together, with the count chosen and held by the analyst, it gives a portfolio map that labels cannot.

Limitations and next steps

  • One universe of 23 ETFs and one of 90 stocks; asset-class and sector labels are a consistency check, not ground truth.
  • The simulation uses one unequal-block design; the gap between methods will vary with block sizes and strengths.
  • Stability is measured on ten 2-year windows only.
  • Next: membership stability weighted by portfolio weight; hierarchical clustering on detoned stock correlations; cluster-based risk budgets against sector budgets out of sample.
